Syringes, Needles and Injection Supplies: Preparing a Request

Syringes and needles look simple to buy, but a vague request can bring the wrong size, the wrong tip or no safety features. Here is what to settle before you ask for a quote.

By the Cloaked Innovations team. Published 7 November 2026. 5 minute read.

Key takeaways

  • WHO recommends syringes with reuse prevention features for all injections, and sharps injury protection wherever possible. [1]
  • Order safety boxes with syringes and needles, because a safe injection should not leave dangerous waste. [1]
  • State volume, tip type, needle gauge and length, sterility, safety feature and pack size on every line.
  • Ask suppliers which standards apply, such as the ISO 7886 series for syringes, and for supporting documents. [3]

Start with injection safety

WHO defines a safe injection as one that does not harm the recipient, does not expose the provider to risk and does not create waste that is dangerous to others. Its providers' guide advises a new needle and syringe for every patient, syringes with a reuse prevention (RUP) feature for all injections, and syringes with sharps injury protection wherever possible. [1]

In 2015 WHO set a policy asking countries to move to safety engineered syringes, where appropriate, by 2020. [2] The safety features take different forms: a metal clip that blocks the plunger, a needle that retracts into the barrel, or a sheath that covers the needle after use. [5] WHO also advises never bending or recapping needles and collecting used sharps in a safety box straight away. [1] That is why safety boxes belong on the same order as the syringes.

What to specify

ItemState in your request
SyringesVolume, tip (Luer slip or Luer lock), with or without needle, safety feature (reuse prevention, auto disable or sharps protection), sterile single use, pieces per box
NeedlesGauge and length, safety feature if required, sterile single use, pieces per box
Safety boxes and sharps containersCapacity and quantity. ISO 23907 covers sharps containers [3]

Standards give you a common language. The ISO 7886 series covers sterile single use syringes, including auto disable syringes (Part 3) and reuse prevention syringes (Part 4), and ISO 23908 covers sharps injury protection features. [3] Quote the standard you want, then ask for the evidence.

Price is part of the decision. When WHO launched its policy, UN agencies were paying 3 to 4 cents for a syringe without safety features and at least double for a safety engineered one. [2] Those figures are from 2015, so ask for current quotations and compare the cost per safe injection, not only per syringe.

Estimating quantities

Because each injection needs a new sterile syringe and needle, start with your injection count: injections per day x days of cover x buffer. As an illustration with made up numbers, a clinic giving 60 injections a day that wants 30 days of cover and a 20 percent buffer needs 60 x 30 x 1.2 = 2,160 syringes and 2,160 needles. At 100 per box, that is 22 boxes of each. Replace these figures with your records, and size safety boxes by sharps volume and collection frequency. Our PPE guide explains the same method.

Documents and storage

Registration of health products and technologies in Kenya is governed by the Pharmacy and Poisons (Registration of Health Products and Technologies) Rules, 2022. [4] Registration requirements depend on the product, so ask each supplier for:

  • The manufacturer, country of origin and registration status where it applies
  • The standards the product meets, with test documents
  • Batch numbers, expiry dates and sterilisation method
  • Storage instructions, and intact sealed packaging on delivery

Common mistakes

  • Leaving out the tip type, so Luer lock syringes arrive when Luer slip was needed
  • Ordering needles without checking they fit the syringes
  • Forgetting safety boxes and other sharps disposal
  • Comparing price per box without checking pieces per box
