Choosing Face Masks for an Organisation

Medical masks, respirators and cloth masks are different products with different standards. This guide shows procurement teams how to tell them apart and what to write in a request.

By the Cloaked Innovations team. Published 3 November 2026. 9 minute read.

Key takeaways

  • Medical masks, respirators and non medical (cloth) masks are three different products and are not interchangeable. [1][7]
  • Medical masks are tested to ASTM F2100 (Levels 1 to 3) or EN 14683 (Types I, II and IIR). Splash resistance is the main difference between them. [1][2][3]
  • For respirators such as N95 or FFP2, check the approval markings and verify them. Counterfeit respirators exist. [5][9]
  • WHO discourages exhalation valves on respirators because they bypass filtration of exhaled air. [1]
  • In your request, state the exact standard and level, the closure type (ear loops or ties), the pieces per box and the documents you need.

Three products, three purposes

Buyers often write "face masks" on a request and receive something that does not match the task. The first step is to know which of the three product groups you need.

ProductWhat it isWhat to look for
Medical mask (surgical or procedure mask)A flat or pleated mask fixed with ear loops or ties. WHO describes medical masks as tested to standardised methods such as ASTM F2100 or EN 14683, balancing filtration, breathability and, optionally, resistance to fluid penetration. [1]ASTM F2100 level or EN 14683 type, with supporting test documents
Respirator (N95, FFP2, FFP3)A tight fitting mask that filters airborne particles. WHO lists N95, FFP2 or FFP3, or equivalent, for care settings where aerosol generating procedures are performed on COVID-19 patients. [1][6]Approval markings and a standard that you can verify, plus fit testing
Non medical (cloth) maskMade of fabric. These masks are not standardised and are not intended for health care settings. [7]Quality varies, so they are not a substitute for medical masks in clinical areas

Which one applies in each area is a decision for your infection prevention and control policy, not for procurement. Our PPE procurement guide explains how to run the risk assessment that supports that decision.

Medical mask standards explained

Two sets of standards dominate. ASTM F2100 is a United States specification with three levels. EN 14683 is a European standard with three types. [2][3] The table below summarises how health authorities describe them.

RatingSplash resistanceTypical purpose in guidance
EN 14683 Type INot splash resistantReducing dispersal of exhaled particles from the wearer [2]
EN 14683 Type IINot splash resistantPreventing microbes from the wearer's nose and mouth reaching a sterile field during a procedure [2]
EN 14683 Type IIRSplash resistant (the R stands for resistant)Moderate to high fluid resistance for health workers [2]
ASTM F2100 Level 1Low (tested at 80 mmHg)General procedures and respiratory etiquette [3]
ASTM F2100 Level 2Moderate (tested at 120 mmHg)Procedures with a moderate risk of splashes [3]
ASTM F2100 Level 3Moderate to high (tested at 160 mmHg)Procedures with a higher risk of splashes [3]

The two systems are not exact equivalents. For example, one Canadian hospital guidance note says a Type IIR mask can stand in for an ASTM Level 1 or 2 mask, but not for Level 3. [3] The practical rule for buyers is to name the exact standard and level you want and to ask for the test documents.

Buying respirators: verify and fit

Respirators protect the wearer only when they are genuine and fitted correctly, so these two checks matter more than price.

Verify the approval

N95 respirators sold in the United States are approved by NIOSH, which requires a testing and certification (TC) approval number on the respirator, with NIOSH in capital block letters. You can check the model and number on the NIOSH Certified Equipment List. [5] NIOSH also warns about counterfeit respirators. Warning signs include no markings, no approval number, a misspelled NIOSH and decorative add ons. [5] Ear loops on a product sold as an N95 are also listed as a warning sign, because genuine N95s use head bands. [9]

FFP2 and FFP3 respirators are covered by European standards. WHO's wording is "N95, FFP2 or FFP3, or equivalent", so ask which standard applies and request the test report. [1]

Plan for fit

A respirator that forms a tight seal needs a fit test, and the wearer should perform a seal check each time it is put on. [5] Fit testing programmes are required by workplace rules in some countries, and they are good practice anywhere. Budget for training and for more than one size or model if your staff need it.

Avoid exhalation valves

WHO discourages exhalation valves on respirators, because they bypass the filtering of exhaled air. [1] Leave valved models out of your specification unless your policy says otherwise.

What to specify in your request

  • Product group: medical mask, respirator or non medical mask
  • Standard and level: for example EN 14683 Type IIR or ASTM F2100 Level 2
  • Closure: ear loops, ties or head bands
  • Size options: adult standard, or small and large where available
  • Pack size and quantity: pieces per box and number of boxes
  • Shelf life: minimum remaining time on delivery
  • Documents: test reports or declarations your institution requires, and the product's registration status in Kenya where it applies [8]

For quantities, use the same method as for other PPE: daily use x days of cover x a buffer. The worked example is in the PPE guide.

Use, storage and common mistakes

Check that each mask is clean, undamaged and dry before use, and train staff in how to put it on, take it off and dispose of it. [1][7] Medical masks are designed to be worn as a single PPE item. A British Columbia infection control position statement notes there is insufficient evidence to support double masking in health care. [4] Store boxes closed and in a dry place, following the manufacturer's instructions.

Common mistakes to avoid:

  • Writing "face masks" without a standard or level
  • Buying cloth masks for clinical areas
  • Accepting respirators without checking markings
  • Ordering a single size of respirator for all staff
  • Layering masks on top of each other

Frequently asked questions

A medical mask is a loose fitting mask tested to standards such as EN 14683 or ASTM F2100. A respirator such as an N95 or FFP2 fits tightly to the face and filters airborne particles. They are used for different purposes under your infection control policy.

Type IIR is an EN 14683 rating for medical masks with splash resistance. The R stands for resistant. Type I and Type II masks are not rated for splash resistance.

Cloth masks are not standardised and are not intended for health care settings. Medical masks or respirators, as your policy requires, are the appropriate choice in clinical areas.

Check for the NIOSH name, a testing and certification number and a filter designation on the respirator, then verify the model and number on the NIOSH Certified Equipment List. Missing markings are a warning sign.
