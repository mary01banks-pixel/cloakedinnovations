PPE Procurement: A Practical Guide for Facilities

How hospitals, clinics, schools and companies in Kenya can plan, specify and buy personal protective equipment with fewer mistakes and fewer emergency orders.

By the Cloaked Innovations team. Published 29 October 2026. 8 minute read.

Key takeaways

  • Build your PPE list from a risk assessment of the tasks your staff perform, not from last year's order. [1]
  • Write a short specification for every item: material, size range, sterile or not, relevant standard, pack size and shelf life.
  • Ask each supplier for product documentation and confirm how the item is registered in Kenya. [5][6]
  • Compare offers on cost per use, availability and delivery time, not only on price per box.
  • Plan storage, expiry tracking and the disposal of used PPE before the order arrives.

What PPE is and why planning matters

Personal protective equipment, or PPE, is wearable equipment such as gloves, masks, gowns and eye protection that protects health workers from exposure to infectious agents and body fluids. The World Health Organization describes standard precautions as the minimum level of infection prevention practice that should be used in the care of all patients, and PPE is one of their key elements, selected on the basis of a risk assessment. [1][2]

When the right item is missing, in the wrong size or expired, staff either go without it or improvise. A procurement plan prevents that. Kenya's Ministry of Health has established national infection prevention and control structures and localised guidance drawn from WHO and CDC recommendations, so your own facility policy and current national guidance should always decide what staff wear for each task. [8] This guide helps you buy; it does not replace that guidance.

Step 1: Start with a risk assessment

WHO advises facilities to train health workers to recognise and assess the risk of exposure to blood, body fluids, secretions, splashes and contaminated surfaces, and to run a risk assessment that reflects the population served and the level of care provided. [1] For procurement, that becomes a simple exercise. For each department or work area, list the tasks and ask:

  • Do staff touch blood, body fluids, mucous membranes or broken skin?
  • Are splashes or sprays likely, for example during cleaning, wound care or procedures?
  • Are patients with respiratory symptoms seen, and does your policy call for masks or respirators?
  • Which tasks need sterile items and which need clean items only?
  • How many staff work each shift, and how often is PPE changed?

The output is a task by task list that your procurement team can turn into an order, instead of a list copied from the last purchase.

Step 3: Work out quantities

Start with real usage. Check stock records or count how many items each area uses in a typical week, then apply this formula:

Order quantity = daily use x days of cover x buffer

Here is an illustration only, using made up numbers. A clinic uses about 300 gloves a day (150 pairs). It wants 30 days of cover with a 20 percent buffer for busy periods. That is 300 x 30 x 1.2 = 10,800 gloves, which is 108 boxes of 100. Your own numbers will differ, so replace each figure with your records.

Remember that suppliers quote in different units. Gloves may be priced per box, per carton or per pair, and masks per box of 50. Always write the unit and the number of pieces in your request so offers can be compared fairly.

Step 4: Write a clear specification

A specification is a short description of exactly what you want. It protects you from receiving a substitute that does not suit the task. For each line item, state:

  • The product name and the use (for example "examination gloves for general wards")
  • Material, size range and whether the item must be sterile
  • The standard or performance level you require, if any
  • Pack size and the quantity you need
  • Minimum remaining shelf life on delivery
  • Delivery location and the date you need the goods

Step 5: Check documents and registration

In Kenya, medical devices are regulated by the Pharmacy and Poisons Board, and registration of health products and technologies is governed by the Pharmacy and Poisons (Registration of Health Products and Technologies) Rules, 2022. [5][6] Registration requirements depend on the product and its classification, so the practical step for buyers is to ask. Before you accept an offer, request:

  • The manufacturer name and country of origin
  • The registration status of the product in Kenya, where it applies
  • Any documents your institution requires, such as test reports or declarations of conformity
  • The batch number and expiry date of the stock being supplied
  • Storage instructions from the manufacturer

A standard printed on a box is a claim, not proof. Ask to see the supporting documents and compare them with your specification.

Step 6: Compare supplier offers

Price matters, but a low unit price can hide a poor match. Compare each offer against the same checklist:

  • Specification match: does the product meet every point you wrote?
  • Cost per use: divide the price by the number of pieces, then compare per glove, per mask or per gown.
  • Documents: are the requested documents provided with the quotation?
  • Availability and lead time: is the stock available now, and how fast can it be delivered to your site?
  • Shelf life: how long before the stock expires?
  • Support: who do you contact about a wrong item or a damaged carton?

Step 7: Plan storage, training and waste

Good procurement does not end at delivery. Follow the manufacturer's instructions for storage and expiry, and train staff to put on and remove PPE correctly. [7] Store older stock at the front so it is used first, and set a reorder level that triggers a new order before you run out. WHO's guidance is to remove and discard PPE when leaving the patient area and to perform hand hygiene afterwards. [1]

Used PPE becomes waste, so plan for it. Make sure each area has suitable bins, bags and sharps containers, and budget for them with the PPE order. You can see related consumables on our products page.

Common procurement mistakes

  • Copying last year's list without checking what staff actually use
  • Ordering by price per box without checking how many pieces are inside
  • Leaving out sizes, so only one glove or gown size arrives
  • Accepting stock with very short remaining shelf life
  • Skipping the request for documents until after delivery
  • Forgetting waste containers and training
