Wound Care Supplies: A Procurement Overview

How hospitals, clinics and organisations can build a sensible wound care range, specify items clearly and compare offers without paying for dressings they do not need.

By the Cloaked Innovations team. Published 3 November 2026. 9 minute read.

Key takeaways

  • Dressing choice is a clinical decision. Procurement's job is to build a core range with your clinical team and to specify each item clearly. [1][2]
  • Dressings generally aim to keep the wound bed moist, absorb excess fluid, protect the wound and address bacterial load. [4]
  • Gauze is inexpensive but needs more frequent changes. Gauze that touches the wound bed should be sterile. [4][5]
  • Choosing dressings arbitrarily can raise cost and the risk of complications, and buying the wrong category wastes money at volume. [2][5]
  • Compare offers on cost per dressing change, not only on price per pack, and always state size, sterility and pack count.

Wound care purchasing covers more than dressings. A clear list helps you check nothing is missing from an order. The table groups common items. The examples we work with include cotton wool, gauze products, crepe bandages and adhesive plasters, and other items are available on request.

Who decides what to buy

Selecting a dressing depends on the wound, the amount of fluid it produces, the stage of healing and the presence of infection. That is a clinical judgment. A review in Podiatry Today makes the point that even the most advanced dressing will not work if the factors that hold back healing are not addressed, and that arbitrary selection can raise cost and complications. [2] Wounds International likewise describes wound healing as a continuum that needs frequent reassessment. [1]

For procurement, the practical approach is to sit down with your wound care lead, senior nurses or clinicians, agree a short list of dressings the facility will stock, and write a specification for each. Revisit the list at least once a year.

Building a core range

A core range should cover wounds with little, moderate and heavy fluid, plus the bandages and tape that hold dressings in place. One supply guide describes the logic this way, and your clinical team should confirm it for your patients: [5]

  • Light to moderate fluid: a hydrocolloid for extended wear, or gauze with more frequent changes when cost or simplicity matters more.
  • Heavy fluid or deep wounds: alginate or hydrofibre packed loosely, covered with a secondary absorbent dressing and secured with a bandage or tape.
  • Dry wounds: a dressing chosen by clinical staff to add moisture.

The same guide warns that buying the wrong category wastes money at volume and can slow healing, for example by sealing a hydrocolloid over a heavily draining wound. [5] Keep the core range small. Every extra product adds training needs and stock to manage.

Sterile or not, and packaging

Sterility matters for items that touch an open wound. A supply selection guide notes that non sterile gauze suits outer layers that do not touch the wound bed, or non wound uses such as padding and cleaning surfaces. [5] For your specification:

  • State sterile or non sterile for every line
  • Ask whether packs are single or multiple, and how many pieces are in each
  • Check that packaging is intact on delivery, and reject damaged packs
  • Record expiry dates and use older stock first

Cost per dressing change and quantities

A cheap dressing that is changed often can cost more over a treatment than a pricier one that stays in place longer. For example, one reference describes changing packed gauze two to three times a day, while other dressing types are changed according to the amount of fluid. [1][4] When you compare offers, calculate the cost per dressing change, and ask your clinical team how often each dressing is changed.

To estimate quantities, count your dressing changes. The formula is: dressing changes per day x days of cover x buffer. Here is an illustration with made up numbers. A clinic performs about 25 dressing changes a day, wants 30 days of cover and adds a 20 percent buffer: 25 x 30 x 1.2 = 900 dressing changes. Split that total across your core range by type and size, using your own records.

Documents, storage and waste

In Kenya, registration of health products and technologies is governed by the Pharmacy and Poisons (Registration of Health Products and Technologies) Rules, 2022. [6] Registration requirements depend on the product, so ask each supplier for:

  • The manufacturer and country of origin
  • The registration status in Kenya, where it applies
  • The sterilisation method and expiry date shown on the pack
  • The batch number and storage instructions
  • Any other documents your institution requires

Used dressings that carry blood or body fluids are health care waste. Safe management of health care waste is part of standard precautions, so budget for bins, biohazard bags and sharps containers with the order. [7]

Common procurement mistakes

  • Buying advanced dressings by default instead of agreeing a clinical range
  • Ordering non sterile gauze for wound contact
  • Comparing price per pack without checking the number of pieces
  • Forgetting secondary dressings, bandages and tape
  • Ordering a single size for every wound
  • Not planning for used dressing waste
