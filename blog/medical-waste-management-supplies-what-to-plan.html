Medical Waste Management Supplies: What to Plan

Waste containers are easy to forget until the first inspection or needle stick injury. Here is how to plan the bins, liners, safety boxes and protective gear a facility needs.

By the Cloaked Innovations team. Published 8 November 2026. 5 minute read.

Key takeaways

  • About 85 percent of health care waste is general and non hazardous, so segregation at the point of use keeps the hazardous share small. [1]
  • Kenyan facility checklists list colour coded pedal bins with liners in black, yellow and red, a safety box and a secure waste holding area. [3]
  • Waste producers are legally and financially responsible for safe handling and disposal. [4]
  • Budget for bins, liners, safety boxes and handler PPE together, and size each by the waste it will receive.

Segregation drives the shopping list

WHO reports that about 85 percent of waste from health care activities is general waste comparable to household waste, while the remaining 15 percent is hazardous and may be infectious, chemical or radioactive. [1] Segregating at the point of use keeps hazardous waste from contaminating the rest, and it reduces the quantity needing special treatment. [2] In practice, that means the general waste bin should be your largest, and every area that produces hazardous waste needs the right container within reach.

What Kenyan guidance expects

Kenya has national Health Care Waste Management Guidelines, updated in 2023. [5] Facility licensing checklists from the Kenya Medical Practitioners and Dentists Council ask for colour coded pedal bins with matching liners in black, yellow and red, plus a safety box, in service areas, and for a secure waste holding area. [3] One referral hospital describes its scheme as black for general waste, yellow for infectious waste, red for highly infectious waste and purple for cytotoxic waste. [6]

National waste regulations are also evolving, including the Environmental Management and Coordination (Waste Management) Regulations, 2024. [7] NEMA guidelines state that waste producers are legally and financially responsible for safe handling and disposal. [4] Colour practice varies between documents, so confirm the current requirements with your county health office or NEMA before you order.

Estimating quantities

Start with the number of service areas, because each needs its own set of containers. For liners, use bins x changes per day x days of cover x buffer. As an illustration with made up numbers, a facility with 8 service areas and 3 bins each has 24 bins. At one liner change a day for 30 days with a 20 percent buffer, that is 24 x 30 x 1.2 = 864 liners. Size safety boxes by sharps volume and how often they are collected, and see our guide to syringes, needles and injection supplies for what goes into them.

Storage, handling and mistakes

WHO advises collecting used syringes and needles in a safety box immediately after use and never bending or recapping needles. [9] Plan a secure holding area, and agree collection and treatment with a licensed handler, since many facilities cannot treat waste on site. WHO favours safe, environmentally sound treatment such as autoclaving over incineration where feasible, so ask your handler how waste will be treated. [1] Label every container with its waste stream so staff and handlers can identify it at a glance. Common mistakes include:

  • Buying one bin colour for every stream
  • Undersizing the general waste bin and overfilling hazardous ones
  • Ordering liners that do not fit the bins
  • Forgetting handler PPE and safety boxes
