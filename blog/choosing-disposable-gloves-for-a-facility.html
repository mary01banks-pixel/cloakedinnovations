How to Choose Disposable Gloves for a Facility

A buyer's guide to examination and surgical gloves: what the types mean, how to read the standards, and what to write in your request so the right gloves arrive.

By the Cloaked Innovations team. Published 29 October 2026. 8 minute read.

Key takeaways

  • Medical gloves fall into two main groups: examination gloves (sterile or not) and surgical gloves. Match the type to the task. [1][7]
  • Gloves are not needed for every task, and unnecessary use wastes resources and can increase the risk of germ transmission. [1]
  • Nitrile, latex and vinyl behave differently. Latex proteins can cause allergic sensitisation, which is why many facilities choose nitrile. [6][8]
  • Standards such as EN 455 and ASTM D6319 set requirements for holes, strength, powder and shelf life. Ask for the supporting test data. [3][4]
  • Always state size range, powder free, sterile or not, and the exact unit (per glove, pair or box) when you request a quote.

The main types of medical gloves

WHO defines medical gloves as disposable gloves used during medical procedures. They include examination gloves, which may be sterile or not, and surgical gloves, which have specific characteristics. [1] In practice a facility buys three groups:

  • Clean examination gloves (not sterile): for contact with mucous membranes, broken skin, or when there is a risk of exposure to blood and body fluids. [7]
  • Sterile gloves: for invasive procedures and contact with sterile sites. [7]
  • Utility or heavy duty gloves: reusable gloves used for cleaning and handling chemicals or waste, a different product from disposable medical gloves. [9]

Buying the wrong group is the most common and most avoidable gloves mistake. Examination gloves are not a substitute for utility gloves in cleaning work, and unsterile gloves cannot replace sterile ones in aseptic procedures.

When gloves are, and are not, needed

WHO's leaflet warns that unnecessary and inappropriate glove use wastes resources and may increase the risk of germ transmission. [1] Gloves also do not replace hand hygiene, which still applies before and after glove use according to the indications in your policy. [1][2] For procurement this has a useful effect: if your staff are trained on when to glove, your usage figures become more accurate and your order becomes smaller and more predictable.

Nitrile, latex or vinyl?

The three common materials differ in comfort, strength and allergy risk. The table below summarises what the sources say, and your facility policy and staff needs decide the final choice.

MaterialPoints to knowConsider when
NitrileSynthetic rubber, no natural rubber latex proteins. Australian infection control guidance describes nitrile as the safest practice for examination gloves. [8]Latex safe environments, general clinical use, tasks with a risk of exposure to body fluids
Latex (natural rubber)Good fit and feel, but residual latex proteins can drive allergic sensitisation. The Australian guidance says latex should be used only when no safer alternative exists, with allergy risks identified and controlled. [6][8]Only where your policy allows and allergy risks are managed
VinylEN 455 sets a lower minimum strength for vinyl (3.6 newtons) than for latex or nitrile (6 newtons). [4]Short, low contact tasks, if your policy allows. Check suitability for the task

Whichever material you choose, specify powder free. Under EN 455, a glove may be called powder free only if it carries no more than 2 mg of powder. [5]

Reading the standards

You will often see standards printed on glove boxes. Here is what the main ones cover.

  • EN 455 Part 1, freedom from holes: a water leak test based on an acceptance quality limit (AQL). Examination gloves use AQL 1.5 and surgical gloves AQL 0.65. A lower AQL is stricter. [3]
  • EN 455 Part 2, physical properties: dimensions and force at break. The minimum is 6 newtons for rubber examination gloves, 3.6 newtons for vinyl and 9 newtons for surgical gloves. [3][4]
  • EN 455 Part 3, biological evaluation: limits on endotoxin, latex proteins, chemical residues and powder, plus labelling rules. [4]
  • EN 455 Part 4, shelf life: tests to support the "best before" date. The maximum claimable shelf life is 5 years from manufacture. [4]
  • ASTM D6319 and ASTM D3578: United States specifications for nitrile and natural rubber latex examination gloves. D3578 also addresses latex protein content. [3][6]

EN 455 and the ASTM specifications cover similar ground, but they are not identical, so read the actual test data rather than assuming equivalence. [10] In Kenya, medical devices are regulated by the Pharmacy and Poisons Board, so also ask the supplier how the product is registered where that applies. [11]

A standard named on a box is a claim. Ask the supplier for the supporting documents and compare them with the requirements in your specification.

Size, fit and pack size

Gloves that are too tight tire the hand and tear more easily, and gloves that are too loose slip and reduce touch. Order a spread of sizes that matches your staff, not a single size. Many facilities start with small, medium and large in the highest quantities and a smaller number of extra small and extra large, then adjust after a month of usage.

EN 455 sets a minimum median length of 240 mm for examination gloves. [5] For your request, also decide:

  • Unit basis: glove, pair or box. Boxes commonly hold 100 gloves, but confirm the count on each quotation.
  • Cuff length: standard or extended, if your tasks need more forearm cover.
  • Colour: some facilities use colour to separate areas or to spot damage.

To compare offers fairly, convert every price to a cost per glove: price per box divided by the number of gloves in the box.

Storage and staff training

Follow the manufacturer's instructions for expiry dates and storage, and train staff to put on and remove gloves correctly. [7] WHO's leaflet includes a donning and removal technique for examination gloves that you can use for training. [1] Keep boxes closed and away from heat and direct sun as the manufacturer advises, and use older stock first. For related planning, see our PPE procurement guide.

Checklist before you order

  • Which departments need gloves, and which tasks in each?
  • Examination, sterile or surgical?
  • Material decided, with latex allergy risks considered?
  • Powder free stated?
  • Size range and quantity per size written down?
  • Unit basis and pieces per box confirmed?
  • Standards and documents requested?
  • Minimum shelf life on delivery stated?

Frequently asked questions

Many facilities choose nitrile because it contains no natural rubber latex proteins, which can cause allergic sensitisation. Latex can still be used where your policy allows and allergy risks are managed.

Vinyl gloves have a lower minimum strength requirement under EN 455 than nitrile or latex. They may suit short, low contact tasks if your policy allows, so check suitability for the task.

AQL is the acceptance quality limit used in sampling tests. For freedom from holes, EN 455 uses AQL 1.5 for examination gloves and 0.65 for surgical gloves. A lower number is stricter.

Boxes commonly contain 100 gloves, but it varies by product. Always confirm the number of gloves per box and compare prices per glove.
