Diagnostic Equipment for Institutional Procurement

A cheap monitor that cannot be serviced, calibrated or supplied with strips costs more than it saves. Here is how to specify and compare diagnostic devices for an institution.

By the Cloaked Innovations team. Published 8 November 2026. 5 minute read.

Key takeaways

  • Equipment needs calibration, maintenance, repair, training and decommissioning, so compare total cost of ownership, not only price. [2]
  • WHO technical specifications can support tendering and purchasing, and planning maintenance. [1]
  • For blood pressure monitors, ask for clinical validation to ISO 81060 Part 2. [3]
  • Pulse oximeter accuracy can be affected by skin pigmentation, poor circulation and nail polish, so ask for clinical test data. [5]
  • For glucose meters, ISO 15197:2013 sets accuracy criteria, and strips are an ongoing cost. [7]

Think beyond the purchase price

The term equipment refers to a medical device that requires calibration, maintenance, repair, user training and decommissioning. [2] That is why buying on price alone is risky. A guide from Management Sciences for Health lists local servicing and spare parts, and local training in use and maintenance, among the main criteria for selecting equipment, and it says specifications should be written with technical staff. [2] WHO's technical specifications give basic, appropriate specifications for low and middle income settings and can be used for tendering, purchasing and planning maintenance. [1]

What to specify, device by device

DeviceState in your request
Blood pressure monitorsManual or automated, upper arm cuff, cuff sizes needed, power source, validation protocol
Pulse oximetersFingertip or handheld, adult and paediatric probes, power, clinical test data
ThermometersDigital or infrared, measurement range, stated accuracy, probe covers, battery type
Glucose metersISO 15197:2013 evidence, test strips and lancets per meter, strip expiry, sample volume

Ask each supplier to quote the full set, including cuffs, probe covers, strips, batteries and chargers, so that offers compare on the same basis. For thermometers, the stated accuracy range is the main figure to compare, so ask how the device should be checked over time. For blood pressure monitors, ask for the range of cuff sizes your patients need.

Asking for evidence of accuracy

Blood pressure monitors

Clinically validated automated arm cuff devices are recommended, and new validation studies should follow ISO 81060 Part 2:2018, often called the universal standard. [3] Several lists of validated devices exist, and STRIDE BP covers blood pressure monitors, glucose monitors and pulse oximeters internationally. [4]

Pulse oximeters

The US FDA warns that readings can be affected by skin pigmentation, poor circulation, skin thickness, skin temperature, tobacco use and nail polish, and that readings should be treated as estimates. [5] It has also proposed that clinical testing include a diversely pigmented group of at least 150 participants. [6] Because many users in Kenya have darker skin, ask manufacturers for test data that covers a range of pigmentation.

Glucose meters

ISO 15197:2013 requires at least 95 percent of results to fall within 15 mg/dl of a laboratory reference below 100 mg/dl, or within 15 percent at 100 mg/dl and above, and 99 percent within zones A and B of the consensus error grid. [7] Ask whether the meter was tested against it, and for the price and expiry of strips.

Warranty, service and training

A WHO laboratory guidance document lists warranty, technical support from the manufacturer and service contracts among the criteria for selecting equipment, and it suggests asking for installation and staff training, an operator's manual and, where possible, a trial period. [8] Put these into your request:

  • Warranty length, and what it covers
  • Local service, spare parts and calibration arrangements
  • Operator training and manuals
  • Registration status in Kenya where it applies, and manufacturer documents [9]

Quantities and requesting a quotation

Count your points of use, then add spares for repair downtime, in a number agreed with your clinical or biomedical lead. As an illustration with made up numbers, 8 consulting and ward points plus 2 spares means 10 units. Add consumables such as cuffs, probe covers and strips to the same order.

Blood pressure machines, digital thermometers, pulse oximeters and blood sugar meters are examples of diagnostic devices on our products page. Send your specification through the contact page and follow our quotation guide. Related guides cover catheters and tubing and waste management supplies.

Example request: "Please quote for 10 automated upper arm blood pressure monitors with adult and large cuffs, 10 fingertip pulse oximeters and 6 glucose meters with 50 strips each. Please state validation evidence, warranty and local service. Delivery to Kisumu."
